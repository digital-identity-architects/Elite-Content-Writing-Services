Ghostwriting a founder on LinkedIn without making them sound like a brand

The tactical playbook from three years of weekly founder interviews. The mistakes cost real reach. The fixes are unglamorous and repeatable.

SR
Sara Reinhart · Senior writer, founder POV
POV

Founder ghostwriting on LinkedIn is the most-faked discipline in B2B. The corner of the internet is full of accounts that all sound like the same person — a person who has recently learned the word contrarian and is excited about it.

They don't get reach because they don't sound like anyone. The platform's algorithm has gotten remarkably good at detecting prose-from-a-template, and the audience got there even faster.

Here's what we've learned ghostwriting for ~30 founders across three years.

The 45-minute interview is the entire job

If you don't have an actual transcript of the founder talking, you don't have a voice to write in. We don't take ghostwriting clients who can't commit to one weekly recorded call, full stop.

That call has rules:

  • No agenda. The minute you give a founder a question list, they answer it the way they'd answer an interview at a conference. Bad source material.
  • Argue with them. Push back on the soft claim. Ask "what's the actual number?" Founders are at their most quotable when defending a position.
  • Record verbatim, edit ruthlessly. 45 minutes of speech becomes 4–6 short posts and one long-form. The job is finding the lines that already work and cutting everything around them.
Ghostwriting is 80% editing a real person down. It is not 80% writing in their voice. The distinction sounds small. It is the entire game.

The four post types that work

We've tested a lot of formats. Four consistently outperform across the founders we write for:

1. The reversal

Founder believed X. Saw evidence Y. Now believes Z. Concrete numbers and a specific decision that changed because of it.

Why it works: it's the only format that proves the founder thinks. The genre most founder content fails is "platitudes about thinking."

2. The unflattering specific

A small thing the founder did wrong this week, what it cost, what they're changing.

Why it works: founders are starved of company so this builds parasocial trust at a rate other formats can't match. Required ingredient: an actual mistake. Inventing one or humble-bragging one ruins it for years.

3. The taxonomy

"There are three types of X. Most people only see two." Useful for category-defining founders. Earns saves and re-shares because readers use it as a thinking shorthand.

4. The decision postmortem

A real decision the company made — pricing, positioning, hiring — and the four or five tradeoffs that drove it. Long-form, not a post.

The mistakes that cost reach

Mistake one: writing the post the founder would write if they had time. They wouldn't. They'd write it cautiously. The reason they hire ghostwriters is to put their actual interesting opinions on the page, edited for clarity.

Mistake two: smoothing the voice out. Founders speak in fragments, with strange emphases and mid-sentence corrections. Most ghostwriters file this off and produce something flatter and less recognizable. We leave the fragments in.

Mistake three: posting on a calendar. Reach drops when there's nothing to say. Twice a week with two real things to say outperforms five times a week with three fillers. Our default cadence is 3 posts and 1 long-form per week, and we skip if the source material isn't there.

Mistake four: the founder approving in batches. Three weeks of unread drafts in a Notion doc kills momentum. We send for approval one piece at a time, usually within an hour of the call, and ask for a 24-hour turnaround.

What the algorithm rewards in 2026

Two things, mostly: conversation in the first hour (so the post needs to invite a specific response, not a generic agreement), and dwell time (so the first three lines have to make scrolling away feel premature).

The hidden third thing is sender consistency: posts from accounts where the comments sound like the same person who wrote the post outperform accounts where they don't. So we coach founders to reply to comments themselves, in their actual voice. This is the one part of the job we can't ghostwrite.

The honest measurement window

Founder LinkedIn takes 90 days to start working and 180 to be obvious. The first six weeks look indistinguishable from doing nothing. We tell every prospect this on the diagnostic call. The ones who don't believe it should not start.

For the ones who do, the compounding is real. Inbound demos, podcast invites, hires, press — they show up around month four and don't stop.
