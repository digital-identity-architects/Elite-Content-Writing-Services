How we charge half what agencies charge — and still make better margins

The unglamorous answer to the question we get on every diagnostic call. It's not offshoring, it's not AI, and it's not a venture subsidy.

EK
Elena Kim · Founding editor
½

The first call with almost every prospective client ends in some version of the same question: "Okay, but how is this priced like that?" They're polite about it. They usually phrase it as a compliment. But under the question is a real concern: what am I about to find out you've been cutting?

Fair. Anyone who's been around content services long enough has been burned by an agency that was suspiciously cheap and delivered exactly what you'd expect from a suspiciously cheap agency.

So this post is the long answer. Three things we do, two things we don't, and the unglamorous math underneath all of it.

What we don't do

Two things, mostly:

We don't run a sales team. Our pipeline is referrals, this blog, and our writers' personal LinkedIns. There is no SDR, no AE, no commissioned closer forwarding you to an account manager. The first email you get from us is from the editor who'll actually do the work.

We don't have an office. Eleven people in four timezones. We meet twice a year, we use Slack, and we expense the coworking days when someone wants them. That's the whole footprint.

Those two line items alone — sales overhead and real estate — are roughly 30–35% of a typical mid-market agency's cost base. They have to charge for them. We don't.

What we do instead

1. Senior-only staffing

This is counterintuitive. Senior writers cost more per hour. But they take a third of the time on a brief, need a fraction of the editing, and ship work the client actually uses on the first pass. Cheaper-per-hour, but more expensive per shipped piece — and usually behind on deadline.

We did the math on this in our first year. A senior writer at $180/hr produces a ready-to-publish 1,500-word piece in 6 hours. A junior at $75/hr produces the same piece — after editor pass, after rewrite, after second editor pass — in 14 hours of combined effort. The senior is cheaper.

The agency model bills hours. The studio model bills outcomes. The math only works one way.

2. The brief is the work

Most agency time disappears into clarification cycles: a junior writer with an ambiguous brief produces something close-but-not-quite, the AM forwards client notes, the writer revises in the wrong direction, two more rounds, ship.

We invest more time per brief — usually 90 minutes of a senior strategist plus 45 minutes of a senior writer — and lose almost zero hours to clarification cycles after it.

3. Async by default

Meetings are the expensive part of agency life. The weekly status, the monthly review, the quarterly business review, the kickoff for each new piece. Each one bills four to seven people for an hour, and in our experience produces about thirty actionable minutes total.

We do one 30-minute call a month per client, plus Slack. That's it. The time the agency would spend in meetings, we spend writing.

The actual math, on a real retainer

Here's our Growth tier ($6,800/month for 10 pieces) broken down:

  • Senior writers (6 hrs × 10 pieces × $180): $10,800 of value
  • Senior editor (1.5 hrs × 10 pieces × $160): $2,400 of value
  • Strategy lead (4 hrs/mo × $200): $800 of value
  • Tools, SERP data, operations (~12% blended): ~$1,200

That's ~$15,200 of fully-loaded internal cost, billed at $6,800. Margin lives in the absence of sales, real estate, and middle management. We're well above industry-typical studio margins on it.

A traditional agency with the same headcount has to add roughly 110–140% on top to cover the org chart. So the same retainer prices out at $14,000–$16,000 there. Same internal cost. Same writers, often. Two and a bit times the invoice.

Why isn't everyone doing this?

The honest answer is that this model only works at small scale and with a very picky client filter. We turn down more work than we take. We can't grow past about 15 active retainers without becoming the thing we replaced. That's the trade-off.

But for our 15 clients, the math works. And the punch-line on every diagnostic call is the same: nothing is being cut. The savings are real. They were just being burned on the org chart all along.
