SEO content that ranks in 2026: a brief written for skeptics

Search has changed more in 18 months than the previous five years. Here's the brief we now hand every senior writer — and why most agency briefs are obsolete.

MA
Marcus Adeyemi · SEO & strategy lead
SEO

Half the SEO briefs floating around the industry right now were written for a search engine that no longer exists. AI Overviews, the rise of ChatGPT-as-search, the slow death of mid-funnel listicles — it's a different SERP.

We rewrote our brief template in late 2024 and have rewritten it three times since. This post is the current version, with the reasoning out loud. Steal it. Adapt it. Pressure-test it on your own writers.

The four-part brief we now use

1. Intent & instance, not keyword & volume

Volume is a vanity number now. Half the searches that show up in your tool are answered inside an AI Overview without a click. The clicks that do happen are disproportionately on terms with high commercial or transactional intent — and on results that read like a person wrote them.

So our briefs lead with two things: what is the searcher actually trying to do (intent), and what specific instance of that intent are we solving (the angle). "How to evaluate vendors" is intent. "How a 12-person team should evaluate observability vendors when they already have a Datadog contract" is the instance. Only the second one ranks anymore.

2. The SERP autopsy

Before we write a word, we read every result currently ranking for the target query. Not skim — read. We tag each one with: format, angle, depth, freshness, and what it's missing. The brief includes the autopsy and an explicit statement of what our piece will do that the current top three don't.

If we can't articulate the gap in one sentence, we don't write the piece.

3. The PoV requirement

Every brief now has a "PoV requirement" line. It says: this piece must contain at least one claim that a competitor would not make.

That's it. The whole line. It looks small but it changes drafts. Hedge-y, sourced-from- other-blogs prose stops being acceptable. Writers have to interview an internal expert or do their own analysis.

Search now rewards the piece that takes a position. The middle of the road is where AI summaries live, and they live there for free.

4. The structure constraint

We constrain structure in two ways:

  • Lede answers the question in 60 words. Search engines and humans both bail otherwise.
  • One scannable artifact per 1,000 words. A table, a numbered checklist, a labeled diagram, a worked example. Walls of paragraphs don't earn re-reads.

What we removed from briefs

Three things we used to include and no longer do:

Keyword density targets. They were always silly; they're now actively harmful. Writers contort prose to hit a number that hasn't moved a ranking in years.

"Related searches" lists. They produce listicle bloat. We now ask the writer to answer the related questions only when an actual reader of the article would naturally have them next.

Word-count minimums. Replaced with: "as long as the angle requires, no longer." Some of our best-performing pieces in the last year are under 800 words.

Two things that actually matter more in 2026

Internal linking is half the lift

We map every new piece into an existing cluster before writing. The brief includes the three to five internal pages it should link to and the anchor text. Pages without an assigned cluster don't get written.

Refresh is a workflow, not a quarterly event

We monitor SERP positions weekly. When a piece slips out of top 5 we have a 10-day refresh SLA: re-read the new top results, identify what changed in intent, rewrite the sections that no longer match. Most pieces need this twice a year. Some need it quarterly.

The skeptic's question

"Doesn't AI overview cannibalize all of this anyway?"

For top-of-funnel definitional content, mostly yes. We've stopped writing it for clients. For PoV pieces, comparison content, technical depth, and anything tied to a real product? Not really. The AI summary often sends the reader to the source — and the source is increasingly the piece that wasn't generic.

The brief above is what you write to be that source.
